Stoicism has seen a surge in popularity over the last two decades, and while that school of philosophy has a lot to give, its rigidity makes it difficult to practice its teachings without the fear of failure. Having read one single book on Stoicism, Epictetus’ *Discourses*, I feel that I’m in a position to take some of the philosophy’s core tenets and repackage them into a less punishing form. I present Stoicism With a Bow on Top.

In the *Discourses*, Epictetus asserts that one must only focus on what one can control, which is one’s own actions and thoughts. This places responsibility on the individual to constantly fight with their impressions, as Epictetus calls them, and to be mindful of their actions.

---

*Discourses 3.12: “An impression is not the thing itself, but only its appearance to the mind.”*

*Essentially the raw appearances/events that we perceive in our daily lives.*

---

Throughout the Discourses, Epictetus repeats this mantra over and over, and rightfully so. For if one can successfully keep their impressions in check, they’ll never act out of accordance with nature and worldly attachments will never cause them distress. In short, they’ll be happy.

However, achieving happiness by Stoic means is an uphill battle. A noble and worthy battle, but uphill nonetheless. Due to Epictetus’ directness in the *Discourses*, it’s easy to view a moment of weakness where an impression gets the better of you as a failure. A true Stoic never falls victim to their impressions or surroundings, right? Unfortunately, a Stoic isn’t built in a day. To his credit, Epictetus doesn’t demand perfection from the get-go. In book 2 chapter 18, he lays out the following strategy to build one’s mental resilience over time:

---

*“If you’re overcome by habit, don’t despair. But after you’ve once been defeated, return to the struggle, and know that the contest is worthy of you… And if you fail again, do the same. For eventually the habit will weaken.”*

---

While this is helpful, most of the *Discourses* consist of Epictetus’ spirited instruction about what not to do. But what if you do do something you’re not supposed to? It’s easy to feel the pressure of the Stoic ideal; this is the development of your character and your pursuit of happiness on the line. It can feel like all is lost after one slip-up.

But wait! While the Sisyphean rock that is the weight of the Stoic ideal *feels* crushing, it isn’t actually crushing you. It’s important to focus on the broader scene: let go of the pain from your “failure” and zoom out. Picture the following: there’s a determined soul, braving a massive boulder and a never-ending hill. For each slip and each step back, the fighter takes one step forward. The fighter even succeeds in pushing the rock up the hill sometimes. Regardless of the direction they move, you see them getting stronger with each moment they wrestle with the stone beast. Now remember, that’s you! That hill is your life, and that rock is Stoicism.

And this is where the bow comes in, courtesy of Natsume Sōseki. In his novel *Kusamakura*, Sōseki’s narrator consistently zooms out, placing what he sees within the frame of a painting or within lines of a haiku. His arduous climb through a mountain rainstorm becomes easier when viewed from an outside perspective. He is no longer a weary traveler, but part of a work of art, symbolizing steadfastness and humility. Just like Sisyphus and like you and I, the narrator struggles. He sets a goal, and labors with no fruit to show for it. But what keeps him going isn’t the outcome, but the journey itself. The narrator recognizes the beauty in the struggle, and recognizes that that’s what makes the struggle worth it in the first place.

So push the rock. Build your strength. Build your discipline. But don’t let one slip cause you to throw away the progress you’ve made and the progress you stand to make. Embody Stoicism, but put a bow on top of it.
